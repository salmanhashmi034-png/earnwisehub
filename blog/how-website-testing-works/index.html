How Website and App Testing Works: A Beginner's Guide

Website and app testing platforms pay people to evaluate digital products and provide feedback. This guide explains how the process works, what testers actually do, and what equipment you need.

By EarnWiseHub Editorial TeamPublished September 8, 2026

Companies invest heavily in making their websites and apps easy to use — but they cannot always see their own products through a user's eyes. That is where user testing comes in. Platforms pay ordinary people to use digital products and share their honest reactions.

What is website and app testing?

Website and app testing (also called user testing or usability testing) involves real people using a digital product while providing structured feedback. This feedback helps product teams identify problems that internal teams often miss.

From a tester's perspective, the process typically involves: navigating to a website or app, following a scenario ("find a pair of trainers and add them to your cart"), speaking your thoughts aloud as you go, and answering follow-up questions. This is recorded — usually your screen plus your voice.

What do testers actually do?

A standard usability test involves:

  1. Reading the scenario: A short description of who you are supposed to be and what task you are completing
  2. Completing the task: Navigating the product and doing what the scenario asks
  3. Thinking aloud: Saying what you are noticing, what is confusing, what you would expect to happen — as you do it
  4. Answering questions: Post-task questions asking for ratings or written responses about your experience

The "thinking aloud" aspect is the most important skill. Testers who narrate clearly and honestly provide the most useful feedback — and tend to receive better ratings.

Equipment you need

For most platforms:

  • A computer or laptop (some platforms support mobile testing)
  • A microphone (built-in is usually acceptable)
  • Screen recording capability (most platforms provide their own recording tool)
  • Reliable internet connection

Types of tests

  • Unmoderated: You complete tasks independently while recording yourself. No researcher is present. This is the most common type for beginner testers.
  • Moderated: A researcher is on a video call while you test. Less common for beginners, but typically pays more.
  • Card sorting: You organise categories or labels to help researchers understand how people mentally organise information.
  • Tree testing: You navigate a simplified version of a site's structure to find items.

Key platforms for testers

  • UserTesting: One of the larger consumer testing platforms. Requires a qualifying test. See our UserTesting review.
  • uTest: A platform covering functional testing alongside user research. See our uTest guide.

Platform availability changes. Always check whether a platform is currently accepting testers from your country before investing time in the application.

What affects your earnings as a tester?

  • Test volume: How many tests are available for your profile
  • Rating/quality score: Platforms rate testers — high-rated testers often access more tests
  • Test type: Moderated sessions typically pay more than unmoderated
  • Your profile demographics: Some demographics receive more targeted tests than others

Realistic earnings

Website testing earnings are supplemental. Most testers earn occasional amounts rather than a consistent weekly income. The actual amount depends heavily on test availability and your acceptance rate. Treat it as occasional income, not a primary source.

Conclusion

Website and app testing is a legitimate, accessible way to earn occasional supplemental income. The skill required is clear verbal communication, attention to instructions and genuine engagement with the product you are testing. It is not a high-income activity, but it is real work that companies genuinely need done.
