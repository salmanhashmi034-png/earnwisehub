Online Earning Scams to Avoid: A Practical Guide

The online earning space attracts scammers who exploit people looking for legitimate income opportunities. This guide covers the most common schemes, how to recognise them, and how to protect yourself.

By EarnWiseHub Editorial TeamPublished September 24, 2026

The more people search for legitimate online income, the more scammers target them. Some scams are crude and obvious. Others are sophisticated and difficult to recognise until you know what to look for. This guide covers the most common categories and the consistent warning signs across all of them.

The universal rule

Before covering specific types, one principle applies to every online earning opportunity:

If it promises significant income with no skills, no experience, no meaningful work, and for a surprisingly small investment — it is not an opportunity. It is a scam or a scam-adjacent scheme.

Real income requires either skills, effort, time, capital, or some combination. Any offer that bypasses all of these is exploiting the hope that there is an exception.

1. Fake job scams

Fake job postings on legitimate job boards or social media platforms mimic real employment. They conduct fake "interviews," make quick job offers, and then either:

  • Ask for payment for "training materials," "equipment," or a "background check fee"
  • Ask for bank details to "set up payroll" and steal money
  • Send you a fraudulent cheque and ask you to forward a portion of it elsewhere

Legitimate employers do not charge fees to hire you. Legitimate employers do not send you money before you start work.

2. Multi-level marketing (MLM) schemes

MLM (multi-level marketing) is a legal business model, but many MLM companies function in practice like pyramid schemes where the vast majority of participants lose money.

Warning signs of MLM schemes that primarily benefit recruiters:

  • Emphasis on recruiting others over selling a product
  • Required upfront "starter kit" purchase
  • Promised high earnings that depend on building a "team"
  • Pressure from existing members to join quickly
  • Income disclosure statements (often buried in documentation) that show the majority of participants earn little or nothing

The US FTC has published guidance on how to distinguish genuine business opportunities from pyramid schemes — available at consumer.ftc.gov.

3. "Dropshipping guru" and "online business" courses

High-priced online courses promising to teach you a specific method to earn thousands per month — typically through dropshipping, Amazon FBA, print-on-demand or similar — proliferate on social media.

The actual issues:

  • The income shown in advertising is typically the income of the person selling the course, not typical students
  • The methods taught are often publicly available information sold at inflated prices
  • The business models taught require significant additional investment, effort and skills beyond what the course provides
  • Many students report little to no income after completing these courses

This does not mean all online business education is fraudulent — but be highly sceptical of any course marketed primarily with lifestyle imagery and income claims.

4. Paid survey scams

While legitimate paid survey and research platforms exist (see our paid research guide), many fake survey sites:

  • Require payment to access surveys
  • Promise unrealistically high earnings per survey
  • Accumulate "points" that never reach a withdrawal threshold
  • Sell your personal data as their actual business model

Legitimate paid research platforms are always free to join. They pay modest amounts per study and are transparent about rates upfront.

5. Cryptocurrency and investment scams

Online investment scams, particularly cryptocurrency-related ones, have become increasingly sophisticated. Common forms:

  • Fake trading platforms: Show you artificial "gains" while taking your money, then disappear when you try to withdraw
  • Romance scams with investment angles: Build a fake relationship online over weeks or months, then introduce an "investment opportunity"
  • "Pig butchering" scams: Similar — build trust over time, then introduce a fraudulent platform
  • Fake celebrity endorsements: Fabricated content claiming a celebrity has endorsed an investment

No legitimate investment guarantees returns. Any investment opportunity introduced via social media, dating apps or unsolicited messages should be treated with extreme caution.

6. Work-from-home assembly and craft schemes

Advertisements for home-based assembly jobs ask you to purchase materials to make products at home, promising payment per item. After you purchase the kit and complete the work, payment is refused on spurious quality grounds. You cannot get your money back because you purchased the kit from the scammers.

Consistent warning signs across all schemes

  • Upfront payment required to access work
  • Guaranteed income claims
  • Pressure to act quickly
  • Income that is disproportionate to the skill or effort involved
  • Vague description of actual work involved
  • Contact through personal messaging apps rather than professional channels
  • Request for personal or financial details early in a process
  • Resistance to answering direct questions about the business model

If you have been targeted

  • Stop all contact immediately
  • Contact your bank if financial information was shared or money was sent
  • Report to the appropriate consumer protection authority for your country (FTC in the US, Action Fraud in the UK, ACCC/Scamwatch in Australia, CAFC in Canada)
  • Report the listing or profile to the platform where you encountered it

Conclusion

Protecting yourself from online earning scams is primarily a matter of applying consistent scepticism to any opportunity that seems too good to be true. Legitimate online income exists and is built around real skills, real work and real value. Any offer that bypasses those fundamentals should be examined carefully before you engage with it.
